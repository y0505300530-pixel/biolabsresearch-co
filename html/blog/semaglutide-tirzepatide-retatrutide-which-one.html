Semaglutide, Tirzepatide, or Retatrutide: Which One Are You Actually Looking At?

These three names show up everywhere right now — and they get mixed up constantly. They sound like versions of the same thing. They're not.

Here's the simple research-facing version of what each molecule is:

Semaglutide — the first widely discussed compound in this family. It engages one receptor pathway (GLP-1). Separate prescription products that use this molecule exist elsewhere; on this site it is listed only as a research reagent.

Tirzepatide — the next-generation molecule in the same conversation. It engages two pathways at once (GLP-1 and GIP). Two mechanisms instead of one.

Retatrutide — the newest of the three. It engages three pathways at once (GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon). It is still in late-stage clinical trials, so there is the most curiosity — and the most confusion — around it.

Why people confuse them

They all belong to the same research family, and online discussion often treats them like “light, medium, and strong.” That is a mistake. They are different molecules with different lengths, different chemical structures, and different publication track records:

SemaglutideTirzepatideRetatrutide
Pathways discussed in literature1 (GLP-1)2 (GLP-1 + GIP)3 (GLP-1 + GIP + glucagon)

That CAS number row matters more than it looks. A CAS number is a molecule’s ID card. When you inquire about research compounds, the supplier should print the exact CAS on the lot documents — if they can’t, you don’t really know what’s in the vial.

What to check before you inquire about any of the three

  • The supplier states which exact molecule you are getting — by name, not just “GLP-1”
  • A lot document (COA) is available and matches the lot number printed on the vial
  • The purity is stated for your specific lot — not “typically 99%” marketing talk
