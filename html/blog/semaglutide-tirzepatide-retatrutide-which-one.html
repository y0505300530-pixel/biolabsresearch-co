Three different CAS numbers, not three versions of one reagent

These three names show up everywhere right now — and they get mixed up constantly. They sound like versions of the same thing. They’re not.

Here’s the simple research-facing version of what each molecule is:

Semaglutide — a GLP-1–pathway analogue (CAS 910463-68-2). On this site it is listed only as a research reagent. A family nickname on a carton is not identity.

Tirzepatide — a different backbone in the same conversation. Literature discusses two pathways at once (GLP-1 and GIP). CAS 2023788-19-2. Two mechanisms instead of one is a chemical difference, not a “strength.”

Retatrutide — a later analogue (LY3437943). Literature discusses three pathways (GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon). CAS 2381089-83-2. It generates the most catalog confusion because the nickname is newest.

Why people confuse them

They all belong to the same research family, and online discussion often treats them like “light, medium, and strong.” That is a mistake. They are different molecules with different lengths, different chemical structures, and different publication track records:

Literature recordExtensively described as a distinct analogueExtensively described as a distinct analogueDescribed mainly in later trial literature (LY3437943)

That CAS number row matters more than it looks. A CAS number is a molecule’s ID card. When you inquire about research compounds, the supplier should print the exact CAS on the lot documents — if they can’t, you don’t really know what’s in the vial.

What to check before you inquire about any of the three

  • The supplier states which exact molecule you are getting — by name and CAS, not just “GLP-1”
  • A lot document (COA) is available and matches the lot number printed on the vial
  • Any purity figure is stated for your specific lot — not a generic marketing line. Certificates are on request.
